## **Purpose**

This SOP explains how the SimpliCreative team delivers a SimpliBlueprint engagement from activation through final strategy and implementation handoff.

SimpliBlueprint is our paid strategic engagement for situations where important questions still need to be answered before implementation can be responsibly defined.

The Blueprint takes us from:

“What appears to be wrong?”

to:

“What does the evidence actually tell us, what matters most, and what should happen next?”

The governing principle is:

`DIAGNOSE BEFORE PRESCRIBE`

We do not enter a Blueprint trying to prove that the client needs a new website, SimpliFoundation, more marketing, or any predetermined tactic.

We enter it with hypotheses.

We leave it with the strongest conclusions, priorities, and recommendations the available evidence can responsibly support.

The Blueprint must also stand on its own. A capable client team or qualified partner should be able to use it without hiring SimpliCreative for implementation.

## **1\. Know When a Blueprint Is Appropriate**

A Blueprint is appropriate when the client has a meaningful marketing or website problem, but important strategic questions remain unresolved.

Those questions may involve:

Business or marketing objectives.

Audience.

Buying committee.

Positioning.

Differentiation.

Offers.

Messaging.

Buyer journey.

Conversion.

Website role.

Content.

Discoverability.

SEO/AEO/GEO.

Credibility and proof.

Measurement.

Technology.

Implementation priorities.

Success criteria.

A Blueprint is not automatically required before Foundation.

If the client's strategy is already sufficiently established and implementation can be responsibly scoped, use the Direct-to-Foundation path.

The question is not:

“Has this client purchased a Blueprint?”

The question is:

“Do we know enough to responsibly determine what should be implemented?”

## **2\. Start With What We Already Know**

A Blueprint should never feel like the client is starting over.

Before asking the client for more information, review what we already have.

This may include:

The Executive Marketing Diagnostic.

Lean Marketing Scorecard™.

Sales Intelligence.

Diagnostic transcript.

CRM and relationship history.

Existing client strategy.

Proposal and SOW.

Website.

Analytics.

Search data.

Existing research.

Brand and messaging materials.

Known partners.

Known stakeholders.

Previous client conversations.

The AI Intelligence Layer should assemble this context before questionnaire generation.

The human strategist should understand the major context, not necessarily manually reread every source.

## **3\. Activate the Blueprint**

Before active strategy work begins, confirm:

| What We Need | Why It Matters |
| ----- | ----- |
| Executed agreement | Confirms engagement |
| Approved scope | Defines our responsibility |
| Client objective | Establishes what we are trying to solve |
| Desired outcome | Establishes what better looks like |
| Why-now context | Explains urgency and business context |
| Diagnostic hypotheses | Gives us questions to investigate |
| Supporting evidence | Shows why those hypotheses exist |
| Contradicting evidence | Prevents premature certainty |
| Known unknowns | Shows what still needs to be learned |
| Relevant client records | Prevents repeated discovery |
| Strategist owner | Establishes responsibility |

At this stage, hypotheses remain hypotheses.

Do not turn the Diagnostic into a conclusion simply because the client has moved into a paid engagement.

---

## **4\. Review the Personalized Questionnaire**

AI generates the first personalized Blueprint Questionnaire from everything already known.

The strategist should review the questionnaire when material judgment is required.

Questions fall into four practical categories:

| Type | What It Means |
| ----- | ----- |
| Confirm | We think we know the answer but need the client to validate it |
| Required | We do not know something necessary for the Blueprint |
| Optional | The answer would improve our understanding but will not block the work |
| Suppress | We already know enough, so do not ask again |

The questionnaire should feel informed.

Instead of asking:

“Who is your target audience?”

when we already have an answer, we might say:

“Based on our previous conversations, here is how we currently understand your primary audience. Please confirm, correct, or expand.”

Do not make the client repeat themselves just because information came from an earlier stage.

---

## **5\. Validate What the Client Gives Us**

When the questionnaire comes back, do not simply append the answers to the client record.

Compare them with what we already know.

Look for:

Confirmed information.

Corrections.

New information.

Conflicting information.

Missing required answers.

New hypotheses.

Changes to existing hypotheses.

New evidence requirements.

A correction is useful intelligence.

If the client tells us something different from what we previously understood, preserve the change rather than silently replacing the old information.

If the conflict could materially affect strategy, the strategist resolves it before treating either version as established fact.

---

## **6\. Decide What We Actually Need to Research**

Do not run every available audit because we have the tools.

Start with the questions.

The research sequence should be:

`Client Objective → Hypotheses → Strategic Unknowns → Evidence Needed → Research`

For each important unresolved question, determine:

What are we trying to learn?

What do we already know?

What evidence would support the hypothesis?

What evidence would challenge it?

Where could that evidence come from?

What does the client need to provide?

What can AI or an approved tool investigate?

What requires human expertise?

What would allow us to reach a conclusion?

This becomes the Blueprint Research Plan.

The Blueprint should be comprehensive enough to answer the client's strategic questions, not comprehensive for its own sake.

## **7\. Gather the Evidence**

Research may draw from different sources depending on the engagement.

Possible sources include:

The client's website.

GA4.

Google Search Console.

CRM.

Sales calls.

Customer interviews or evidence.

Voice of Customer material.

Search behavior.

SparkToro.

Competitive research.

AI recommendation research.

Existing content.

Brand and messaging materials.

PR and authority signals.

Technology documentation.

Campaign information.

Client documents.

Other approved evidence.

The goal is not to create a collection of separate platform reports.

We are gathering evidence to answer strategic questions.

---

## **8\. Check the Quality of the Data**

Before relying heavily on data, determine whether it is actually usable.

For example:

Are meaningful GA4 conversions configured?

Are UTMs consistent?

Does the CRM have reliable source data?

Does everyone use the same definition of a qualified lead?

Are opportunity values complete?

Is Search Console connected to the correct property?

Is there enough historical data to identify a meaningful pattern?

Is third-party audience data appropriate for this particular question?

We use five practical data-quality levels:

| Quality | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- |
| Reliable | Strong enough for the intended analysis |
| Usable With Limitations | Useful, but important limitations exist |
| Directional | Can inform thinking but should not carry major conclusions alone |
| Insufficient | Cannot responsibly answer the question |
| Unavailable | We do not have the data |

Poor data is itself useful information.

It may reveal a measurement problem that belongs in the Blueprint.

---

## **9\. Look Across the Evidence, Not Just at Individual Platforms**

One of the most important parts of the Blueprint is connecting evidence.

Analytics may tell us where attention originates.

Search Console may tell us what people search.

CRM may tell us which inquiries become opportunities.

Sales conversations may tell us what buyers actually care about.

Audience research may tell us where relevant people spend attention.

The website may show us what buyers encounter when they arrive.

Those pieces become more useful when viewed together.

For example, instead of reporting:

“Organic traffic declined 18%.”

we want to understand:

Which pages declined?

Which queries declined?

Which buyer intents were affected?

Were those pages producing meaningful actions?

Did qualified demand change?

Did pipeline change?

What does that mean for the client's actual objective?

The platform metric is evidence.

The Blueprint needs the strategic meaning.

---

## **10\. Treat Conflicting Evidence as Useful**

Sometimes the evidence will disagree.

That does not mean we should choose the source we like better.

For example:

The client may describe one primary buyer while CRM data shows another pattern.

Analytics may attribute conversions to a channel that CRM data does not.

Search traffic may be strong while qualified inquiries remain weak.

Audience research may suggest a source matters while customer evidence does not show the same influence.

When evidence conflicts, ask:

Why might these sources disagree?

Is one source more reliable for this particular question?

Is there missing information?

Does the disagreement expose a larger problem?

What else would we need to know?

Conflicting evidence often reveals something strategically important.

---

## **11\. Validate the ICP and Buying Context**

Use the evidence available to determine whether the client's current understanding of the buyer holds up.

Consider:

Who appears to be buying?

Who initiates research?

Who uses the service?

Who influences the decision?

Who approves it?

What triggers the search?

What problems are buyers trying to solve?

What outcomes matter?

What criteria shape the decision?

What objections appear?

What language do buyers use?

What evidence makes them trust the organization?

Do not redefine the ICP because one tool produces an interesting audience pattern.

Client evidence, customer evidence, CRM evidence, sales evidence, search evidence, and third-party research should be interpreted together.

---

## **12\. Analyze Positioning and Differentiation**

Determine whether the organization is easy for the right buyer to understand and distinguish.

Look at:

How the company describes itself.

How customers describe it.

How competitors position themselves.

What buyers compare.

Why clients choose the company.

What sales hears repeatedly.

What proof supports the claims.

What the company wants to be known for.

Where the current story becomes generic or confusing.

The goal is not clever language.

The goal is strategic clarity.

---

## **13\. Develop the Storytelling & Messaging Architecture**

Use the approved Storytelling Framework to organize what the evidence tells us about the buyer and the client's role.

Clarify:

Who the primary buyer is.

What they want.

What functional problem they face.

How that problem affects them personally.

Why the situation feels unacceptable.

What underlying conflict is creating friction.

What the client believes about the problem.

Why the client is credible.

What path forward the client provides.

What action the buyer should take.

What happens if nothing changes.

What success looks like.

Who the buyer becomes after the problem is resolved.

What central idea makes the story memorable.

The framework helps organize the strategy.

It should not force evidence into a predetermined narrative.

---

## **14\. Evaluate the Website Through FAST**

FAST helps us determine whether the website is functioning as the digital foundation the marketing ecosystem needs.

### **Flexible**

Can the marketing team actually use the website?

Look at editing capability, CMS limitations, landing-page creation, reusable components, developer dependencies, approval friction, and the team's ability to move at the speed of the business.

### **Accessible**

Can people effectively access and use the experience?

Use appropriate automated and human evidence.

Do not treat an automated accessibility scan as a legal compliance determination.

### **Strategic**

Does the website help the right buyers find, understand, trust, and choose the organization?

Look at audience alignment, messaging, positioning, buyer journey, discoverability, credibility, content, offers, CTAs, and ecosystem alignment.

### **Trackable**

Can meaningful interactions be measured?

Look at conversion definitions, analytics, CRM, funnel stages, attribution capabilities, reporting, and measurement gaps.

FAST is a website-quality and implementation framework.

It is not the entire Blueprint.

---

## **15\. Evaluate Discoverability**

Determine whether the organization can be found in the places and situations that matter to its buyers.

Depending on the engagement, this may include:

Traditional search.

Search intent.

Content visibility.

Authority.

Third-party sources.

AI-assisted research.

AEO/GEO.

Competitive visibility.

Brand demand.

The goal is not simply “more traffic.”

We want to understand whether the organization is visible for the right problems, categories, questions, and buying situations.

---

## **16\. Use AEO Research Carefully**

Where relevant, use validated buyer information to create realistic synthetic research situations.

Ask the kinds of questions a buyer might reasonably ask an AI system while researching the problem, category, or providers.

Then examine:

Which brands appear?

Why do they appear?

Which competitors recur?

Which criteria seem to influence recommendations?

Which external sources recur?

Where is the client represented strongly?

Where is the client absent?

What authority, content, evidence, or source opportunities appear important?

Treat these as research tests.

They are not proof of actual individual buyer behavior.

They are not evidence of proprietary AI training data.

They do not guarantee future AI visibility.

---

## **17\. Evaluate the Buyer Journey and Conversion Path**

Look at what happens from discovery through meaningful business action.

Depending on the client, meaningful actions might include:

Qualified inquiries.

Consultations.

Appointments.

Applications.

Registrations.

Memberships.

Donations.

Enrollment.

Opportunities.

Other appropriate actions.

Ask:

Where does attention originate?

Where do buyers enter?

What do they need next?

Where does trust need to increase?

Where do buyers hesitate?

What actions are available?

What happens after conversion?

Where does the journey break?

Do not reduce conversion analysis to buttons and forms when the underlying issue may be positioning, proof, buyer fit, content, or the offer itself.

---

## **18\. Evaluate Measurement**

Determine what the client can currently prove about marketing performance.

Look at:

Meaningful conversions.

Funnel stages.

Website actions.

Lead qualification.

Opportunity creation.

Revenue connection where defensible.

Analytics.

CRM.

Attribution.

Leadership reporting.

Baseline metrics.

Data gaps.

Ask:

Can the client connect attention to meaningful action?

Can they connect meaningful action to downstream business outcomes?

Where does that chain break?

What would leadership actually need to see?

The goal is to make marketing's impact visible.

Do not claim ROI where the available revenue, cost, and attribution evidence cannot support it.

---

## **19\. Validate the Original Hypotheses**

Return to the hypotheses that entered the Blueprint.

For each important hypothesis, decide whether the evidence:

Validates it.

Partially validates it.

Disproves it.

Leaves insufficient evidence.

Do not make the Blueprint look cleaner by deleting hypotheses that turned out to be wrong.

A disproved hypothesis is useful because it prevents the client from spending time and money solving the wrong problem.

---

## **20\. Determine Root Causes and Contributing Conditions**

Once the evidence has been analyzed, determine what appears to be creating the observed friction.

The reasoning should move through:

`Symptom → Hypothesis → Evidence → Validation → Root Cause or Contributing Condition`

Do not force every situation into one root cause.

Complex marketing problems often have multiple contributing conditions.

Be especially careful with causal language.

If the evidence shows association but not causation, say so.

The strategist owns the final interpretation.

---

## **21\. Prioritize What Matters**

Not every finding deserves immediate action.

Prioritize recommendations as:

| Priority | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- |
| Fix Now | Material issue that should be addressed first |
| Fix Next | Important, but dependent on higher-priority work or appropriate for the next phase |
| Monitor | Worth watching but does not currently justify action |
| Do Not Prioritize Yet | Evidence or business value does not currently justify investment |

Priority should reflect business impact, evidence strength, strategic importance, dependencies, implementation sequence, client capacity, risk, and measurement implications.

Do not simply rank the longest list of problems.

The Blueprint should create focus.

---

## **22\. Build Recommendations From Evidence**

Every important recommendation should answer:

What did we find?

What evidence supports it?

Why does it matter?

What appears to be creating the problem?

What should change?

How important is it?

What must happen first?

What will implementation require?

Who may need to own it?

How will we know whether it helped?

If we cannot connect a recommendation to sufficient evidence, we are not ready to prescribe it.

Keep investigating, qualify the recommendation appropriately, or leave the question unresolved.

## **23\. Build the Implementation Blueprint**

The strategy should become actionable.

For each material workstream, clarify:

What needs to happen.

Why.

Priority.

Dependencies.

Implementation requirements.

Relevant owner.

Required client decisions.

Measurement requirements.

Success measures.

Known constraints.

Remaining unknowns.

The client should be able to hand the Blueprint to their internal team or another qualified partner and understand what needs to happen next.

---

## **24\. Apply the Standalone Value Test**

Before finalizing the strategy, ask:

“If SimpliCreative did not implement this work, could a capable client team or qualified partner understand what needs to happen, why it matters, what should happen first, and how progress should be evaluated?”

If the answer is no, the Blueprint is not finished.

Do not intentionally leave strategic gaps that force the client into SimpliFoundation.

The Blueprint Report contains the depth behind the strategy.

Depending on the engagement, it may include:

Executive summary.

Root causes and contributing conditions.

Strategic priorities.

The Report should reflect the analysis the client actually needed.

---

## **26\. Prepare the Strategy Deck**

The Strategy Deck is not a shorter Blueprint Report.

Its job is to help executives understand the strategic story.

The narrative is:

`WHAT WE FOUND → WHY IT MATTERS → WHAT HAPPENS NEXT`

AI can generate the first draft from approved Blueprint findings, evidence, priorities, recommendations, and implementation requirements.

The strategist then edits it.

Use insight-driven headlines.

Keep one primary idea per slide.

Show enough evidence to support the conclusion without turning the deck into a research archive.

The deck should never introduce a new root cause, recommendation, priority, or claim that does not exist in the Blueprint reasoning.

---

## **27\. Prepare for the Strategy Session**

Before meeting with the client, the strategist should be able to clearly explain:

What we set out to understand.

What evidence we reviewed.

What we found.

What surprised us.

What hypotheses were validated or disproved.

What appears to be causing the friction.

What matters most.

What we recommend.

What should happen first.

What remains uncertain.

What decisions we need from the client.

How progress should be measured.

The strategist should also know where deeper evidence lives if the client asks.

The Strategy Deck leads the conversation.

The Blueprint Report supports it.

---

## **28\. Lead the Strategy Session**

The Strategy Session is a strategic conversation, not a document walkthrough.

Start with the client's objectives and the problem the engagement was designed to answer.

Then guide the client through the most important findings, implications, priorities, and recommendations.

Explain evidence and limitations when they matter.

Do not overwhelm the client with every data point collected.

The goal is understanding and decision-making.

Capture:

Questions.

Corrections.

Disagreements.

Decisions.

Changed assumptions.

Changed priorities.

Implementation considerations.

Remaining unknowns.

The strategist owns the conversation.

AI does not.

---

## **29\. Process Client Feedback**

After the Strategy Session, distinguish between:

A factual correction.

New evidence.

A client preference.

A strategic disagreement.

A changed business condition.

An implementation decision.

A scope consideration.

A request for clarification.

Do not automatically change a recommendation because the client disagrees.

Understand why.

If the client provides new evidence, evaluate it.

If the issue is preference, preserve that distinction.

If the client makes an authorized strategic decision, record it.

---

## **30\. Finalize the Blueprint**

After the Strategy Session:

Update findings where required.

Update evidence where required.

Update priorities.

Update recommendations.

Update implementation requirements.

Update measurement requirements.

Capture client decisions.

Preserve unresolved questions.

Reconcile the Blueprint Report.

Reconcile the Strategy Deck.

Make sure the two tell the same strategic story.

Human approval is required before the Blueprint becomes final.

---

## **31\. Decide the Implementation Path**

After the Blueprint, there are two primary paths:

`CLIENT / PARTNER IMPLEMENTATION`

or

`SIMPLIFOUNDATION`

Client / Partner Implementation means the client implements internally or with another qualified partner.

This is not a SimpliCreative offer.

SimpliFoundation is appropriate when the client wants SimpliCreative to execute the custom implementation and the engagement is a fit.

Do not frame Foundation as necessary for the Blueprint to have value.

The Blueprint has already done its job.

---

## **33\. Hand Off to the Client or Partner**

If SimpliCreative is not implementing, make sure the Blueprint can still be used.

The client or partner should be able to understand:

What should happen.

Why.

Priority.

Dependencies.

Implementation requirements.

Strategic decisions.

Measurement requirements.

Known limitations.

Unresolved questions.

## **34\. Use AI as an Intelligence Partner**

During Blueprint, AI is useful for:

Assembling existing client context.

Generating the personalized questionnaire.

Prefilling known answers.

Identifying missing information.

Planning research.

Retrieving approved data.

Organizing evidence.

Synthesizing across sources.

Identifying contradictions.

Drafting analytical records.

Testing hypotheses against evidence.

Preparing first drafts of Blueprint sections.

Preparing the first Strategy Deck draft.

Organizing client feedback.

Checking traceability.

Identifying unsupported claims or conclusions.

AI does not own the strategy.

People decide what the evidence means, what matters, what should be recommended, and what the client should do.

---

## **35\. Know What AI Must Not Decide**

Do not delegate final judgment to AI when determining:

The client's root cause.

Final strategic recommendations.

Final priorities.

Positioning.

Material changes to ICP.

Final implementation path.

Scope.

Pricing.

Client commitments.

Accessibility compliance.

Guaranteed SEO/AEO/GEO outcomes.

Guaranteed traffic, conversion, pipeline, or revenue outcomes.

Final Blueprint approval.

If AI sounds certain, that does not make the evidence stronger.

---

## **36\. Preserve the Reasoning**

A good Blueprint should make it possible to understand why a recommendation exists.

For important recommendations, we should be able to trace:

`Client Objective → Symptom → Hypothesis → Evidence → Analysis → Validated Finding → Root Cause / Contributing Condition → Priority → Recommendation → Implementation Requirement → Success Measure`

This protects the strategy from becoming a collection of opinions.

It also gives Foundation the context it needs later.

Client decisions.

---

## **39\. Learn Without Turning One Blueprint Into Agency Truth**

A Blueprint may reveal an interesting pattern.

Capture it as a potential learning when appropriate.

Do not turn a client-specific finding into a SimpliCreative methodology rule simply because it worked once.

Agency learning requires broader evidence and deliberate human approval.

A client teaches us about that client first.

Only repeated, validated patterns should influence agency-level knowledge.

## **Blueprint Delivery at a Glance**

| Phase | Team Focus | Done When |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Activate | Assemble what we know and define the engagement | Valid Blueprint starting point exists |
| Validate | Confirm and complete client context | Required inputs are reliable enough to proceed |
| Plan Research | Identify what we actually need to learn | Research Plan answers strategic unknowns |
| Gather Evidence | Retrieve relevant evidence | Required evidence is available or limitations are known |
| Analyze | Connect evidence across strategic questions | Findings are sufficiently supported |
| Validate Hypotheses | Test what we thought was happening | Hypotheses are validated, disproved, qualified, or unresolved |
| Diagnose | Determine root causes and contributing conditions | Strategist approves interpretation |
| Prioritize | Determine what matters first | Client has a focused priority sequence |
| Recommend | Translate findings into action | Recommendations trace to evidence |
| Build Blueprint | Create implementation-ready strategy | Report stands on its own |
| Prepare Deck | Build executive strategic narrative | Strategist-approved presentation is ready |
| Strategy Session | Create understanding and decisions | Client understands the strategy and decisions are captured |
| Finalize | Reconcile feedback and decisions | Final Blueprint is approved |
| Route | Determine implementation path | Client / Partner Implementation or SimpliFoundation is clear |

The standard for a Blueprint is not “Did we produce a comprehensive strategy document?”

It is:

“Did we understand the problem deeply enough to stop guessing, determine what the evidence actually supports, identify what matters most, and give the client a clear path they can confidently execute?”
