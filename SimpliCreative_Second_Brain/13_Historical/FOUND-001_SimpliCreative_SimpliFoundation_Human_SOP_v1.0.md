## **Purpose**

This SOP explains how the SimpliCreative team delivers a SimpliFoundation engagement from kickoff through launch and closeout.

SimpliFoundation is our custom implementation engagement. It turns approved strategy into a working digital foundation.

The principle behind the work is simple:

```
STRATEGY MUST SURVIVE EXECUTION
```

A strong strategy loses its value if the reasoning behind it disappears once design and development begin.

Foundation is therefore not just a website production process. Every major implementation decision should connect back to what we are trying to accomplish for the client, who we are trying to reach, and what the approved strategy says needs to change.

AI can help us work faster and retain context, but people remain responsible for the work and the decisions.

## **1\. How a Foundation Engagement Begins**

A Foundation can begin in one of two ways.

### **After a SimpliBlueprint**

The client has completed a Blueprint and has chosen SimpliCreative to implement the approved strategy.

The Blueprint is our primary strategic starting point.

We should not restart discovery or ask the client to explain information they have already given us unless something needs clarification or has changed.

### **Direct-to-Foundation**

The client already has enough validated strategy for us to confidently define the implementation.

In this case, the approved Foundation scope and validated client strategy become our starting point.

A Blueprint is not required simply because we are building a custom website.

### **Before We Start**

The Project Lead confirms that the engagement is ready for Foundation and that we have:

| What We Need | Why We Need It |
| ----- | ----- |
| Signed agreement and approved scope | Everyone understands what we are responsible for |
| Clear business, marketing, and website objectives | The team understands what success should support |
| Approved strategy | We know what governs implementation |
| Primary audience | We know who the experience is for |
| Known stakeholders | We know who is involved in decisions |
| Known partners | We understand responsibilities and dependencies |
| Known constraints | We understand timing, technology, budget, capacity, and other limitations |
| Required access | The team can perform the work |
| Project owner | Someone is accountable for moving the engagement forward |
| Known open questions | The team knows what is still unresolved |

Do not begin major implementation work if we cannot identify the strategy that is supposed to guide it.

## **2\. Prepare the Strategy-to-Execution Handoff**

Before the full team begins implementation, the Strategist and Project Lead prepare the information the delivery team needs.

When Foundation follows a Blueprint, most of this should already exist.

The handoff should make it easy for the team to understand:

What is the client trying to accomplish?

What marketing problem are we helping solve?

What role should the website play?

Who is the primary buyer?

What does that buyer need to understand, believe, trust, and do?

What is the approved positioning?

What differentiates the client?

Which offers matter most?

What is the approved messaging direction?

What does the buyer journey look like?

What are the primary conversion objectives?

What did the Blueprint identify as the most important findings?

What are the approved recommendations?

What needs to happen first?

What dependencies exist?

What has the client already decided?

What constraints do we need to work within?

How will progress and impact be measured?

What is still unresolved?

The delivery team should not have to reconstruct these answers from meeting transcripts, old emails, the proposal, or scattered files.

## **3\. Hold the Internal Foundation Kickoff**

The internal kickoff aligns the people who will actually execute the work.

This is not a presentation of every page of the Blueprint.

Focus on what the delivery team needs to know to make good decisions.

Review the client objectives, audience, strategic direction, major findings, priorities, scope, implementation requirements, dependencies, constraints, open questions, and responsibilities.

By the end of the kickoff, each team member should understand how their work contributes to the larger strategy.

If the developer understands only the technical requirements, or the designer understands only the sitemap, the handoff is incomplete.

## **4\. Assign Responsibilities**

Responsibilities vary by engagement, but ownership should always be clear.

| Role | Typical Responsibility |
| ----- | ----- |
| Founder / CEO | Executive strategic judgment and major strategic decisions |
| Principal | Client relationship, project leadership, scope context, communication, and delivery decisions |
| Operations / Website Support | Coordination, website administration, implementation support, content support, and QA |
| Lead Developer | Technical architecture, development, integrations, technical quality, and launch readiness |
| WordPress Support | WordPress implementation, content population, component implementation, website changes, support, and QA |
| Strategist | Strategy interpretation, messaging, requirements, strategic review, and strategic fidelity |
| Client | Required inputs, feedback, decisions, approvals, and client-owned responsibilities |
| External Partners | Work specifically assigned to them within the engagement |

The Project Lead should clarify ownership when the engagement requires responsibilities outside these typical roles.

Do not assume responsibility solely from someone's title.

Once the team understands the strategy, translate it into the actual work required.

Depending on scope, Foundation may include:

Messaging and editorial guidance.

Information architecture.

Website content.

UX.

Design.

Development.

CMS configuration.

Forms.

CRM integrations.

Conversion paths.

SEO.

AEO/GEO.

Schema.

Analytics and measurement.

Accessibility.

Redirects.

Technical performance.

QA.

Launch.

Not every Foundation includes every workstream.

The SOW determines what we have agreed to deliver.

The strategy determines how the work should be approached.

## **6\. Know When to Keep Moving and When to Stop**

Implementation always reveals new information.

Not every discovery requires reopening strategy.

If the question is about how to execute an already approved requirement, the appropriate specialist can usually make the implementation decision.

For example, choosing how to build a component, determining a responsive behavior, or selecting the appropriate technical implementation may simply be part of execution.

Stop and bring the issue back to the Strategist when the new information could materially change:

The primary audience.

Positioning.

Differentiation.

Offers.

Messaging strategy.

Buyer journey.

Site architecture.

Conversion strategy.

Discoverability strategy.

Measurement strategy.

What we are building.

Why we are building it.

What should happen first.

How success is defined.

Do not quietly solve a strategic question inside design or development.

Likewise, if new work appears to be outside the approved scope, bring it to the Project Lead before absorbing it into the project.

## **7\. Use Research Only When It Answers a Real Question**

Foundation may require additional research, but we should not rerun the Blueprint simply because implementation has started.

Additional research makes sense when:

Prior evidence is stale.

A new implementation question emerges.

A technical decision requires evidence.

The client provides new information.

An assumption is challenged.

Sources contradict each other.

The actual implementation environment differs from what we previously understood.

Start with the question.

Then determine what evidence is required to answer it.

AI can help perform and synthesize approved research, but a human interprets findings that affect strategy or implementation.

## **8\. Develop Messaging When It Is Part of Scope**

Messaging should begin with the approved strategy, not a blank page.

Use the approved buyer, positioning, differentiation, offers, Storytelling & Messaging Architecture, Voice of Customer evidence, proof, and client decisions as the foundation.

AI can create a first structured draft.

The Strategist then evaluates whether the messaging accurately expresses the approved strategy and makes it clearer, more specific, and more useful.

Client review happens when required.

Do not accidentally create a new strategy because a different headline or positioning statement sounds stronger.

If the messaging work suggests that the approved positioning, audience, offer, or strategic narrative needs to change, bring that decision back to the Strategist.

## **9\. Create Client Editorial Guidance When Needed**

When included in scope, translate the approved messaging into practical guidance the client and team can use consistently.

This may include voice, tone, preferred vocabulary, terminology to avoid, writing principles, claim standards, editorial mechanics, audience considerations, and useful examples.

AI can prepare the first draft.

A human edits and approves the guidance.

Durable client preferences that will affect future work should be captured so the team does not have to relearn them on every project.

## **10\. Develop the Information Architecture**

Information architecture should reflect the strategy rather than simply reproduce the client's current website.

Consider:

Who the buyer is.

What they need.

How they move through the decision process.

What the client needs to communicate.

Which offers matter.

What proof buyers need.

What content exists.

What needs to be discoverable.

What actions matter.

What search equity or existing content should be preserved.

What technical and operational constraints exist.

The sitemap is a strategic implementation decision and remains human-led.

## **11\. Define What Each Important Page Needs to Do**

Before substantial copy or design work begins, the team should understand the job of each important page.

For each material page, clarify:

Who is this page for?  
 Why does it exist?  
 What is the buyer’s primary intent?  
 What should the visitor understand?  
What do they need to believe or trust before taking the next step?  
 What should they do?  
 What message matters most?  
 What proof is required?  
 What content is required?  
 What functionality is required?  
 What discoverability requirements apply?  
 What needs to be measured?  
 How will we know whether this page is performing its intended strategic job?  
 What dependencies exist?

Buyer intent should inform the job of the page, the depth of information required, the proof a buyer needs, and the appropriate next action.

Not every page should attempt to convert a visitor directly into a sales conversation. Match the page to the buyer’s current level of awareness and give them the next logical step.

| Buyer intent | What they’re thinking | Page type | Primary job of the page | CTA style | Do not force |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Unaware | “I don’t know there’s an issue yet.” | Thought-leadership article, trend report, executive guide | Introduce a shift, problem, or new idea | Read, explore, download | A sales conversation before relevance exists |
| Symptom Aware | “Something isn’t working, but I don’t know why.” | Educational article, guide, educational landing page | Help them recognize and understand the symptoms | Learn more, continue exploring | A specific solution before the problem is understood |
| Problem Aware | “I think I know what the problem is. Do I actually have it?” | Diagnostic, assessment, quiz, problem-focused landing page | Define and help diagnose the problem | Take assessment, get score, explore diagnosis | A predetermined solution before diagnosis |
| Solution Aware | “I know the problem. How can it be solved?” | Methodology/framework page, solution page, comparison page | Explain the approach and build belief in the solution | See how it works, explore solution | Immediate purchase when evaluation is still needed |
| Product Aware | “I know solutions like yours exist. Why you?” | Service page, sales page, case study, offer landing page | Differentiate the offer and prove credibility | Book, buy, request proposal | Introductory education that unnecessarily delays evaluation |
| Most Aware | “I’m interested. Give me a reason to act now.” | Conversion page, booking page, proposal/offer page | Remove final objections and make action easy | Book, start, purchase | Unnecessary educational steps or additional friction |

A website may serve buyers at multiple intent levels, and an individual page may support more than one stage. Identify the page’s primary buyer intent before defining its message hierarchy, proof, content depth, and CTA.

Secondary pathways may support adjacent intent levels, but they should not compromise the page’s primary job.

This framework is a planning tool. It does not mean the website needs a separate page for every buyer-intent stage. Page architecture should still be based on the approved strategy, buyer journey, evidence, scope, information needs, and implementation requirements.

Use the following sequence when defining page requirements:

`BUYER_INTENT → PAGE_JOB → CONTENT_DEPTH → PROOF_NEED → CTA`

Apply these rules:

`PAGE_JOB_MUST_MATCH_BUYER_INTENT`

`CTA_MUST_MATCH_BUYER_INTENT`

`DO_NOT_FORCE_LATE_STAGE_CONVERSION_ON_EARLY_STAGE_INTENT`

`DO_NOT_ADD_EDUCATIONAL_FRICTION_WHEN_BUYER_IS_READY_TO_ACT`

AI can help assemble page requirements from the approved strategy and known buyer journey.

The responsible human validates the primary buyer intent, page job, required proof, CTA, and other material requirements before the page moves into execution.

## **12\. Draft and Approve Website Content**

When copy is included, work from the approved strategy, messaging, page requirements, editorial guidance, and proof.

The working sequence is:

```
Approved Strategy
→ Approved Messaging
→ Page Requirements
→ Approved Proof
→ First Draft
→ Editorial Review
→ Client Review
→ Approved Copy
```

AI can create first drafts.

A human is responsible for making those drafts strategically sound, specific, useful, accurate, and appropriate for the client's voice.

Before client review, check AI-assisted copy for repetitive structure, shallow observations, formulaic language, unnecessary contrast framing, and other patterns defined in SimpliCreative's editorial standards.

SimpliCreative avoids em dashes in brand content unless a human deliberately approves an exception.

Never invent customer results, statistics, testimonials, credentials, capabilities, guarantees, buyer quotations, proof, or unsupported differentiators.

Reference material may inform thinking. It is not a copy bank.

## **13\. Prepare the Creative Brief**

Before design begins, give the designer enough context to understand what the design needs to accomplish.

The Creative Brief should communicate the design objective, audience, approved positioning, messaging hierarchy, buyer journey, conversion objectives, proof requirements, brand standards, page requirements, accessibility requirements, functional requirements, client preferences, constraints, and relevant approved strategy.

AI can help assemble the first draft.

The Strategist and Designer decide whether the brief provides enough context to begin.

A designer should not have to reverse-engineer strategy from copy documents or meeting notes.

## **14\. Design the Experience**

UX and visual design are human-led.

Design should help the right people:

Understand the organization.

Recognize relevance.

Navigate the experience.

Trust the organization.

Find the information they need.

Take the appropriate next action.

Design decisions should also account for accessibility, content hierarchy, responsive behavior, functionality, brand expression, conversion, and implementation feasibility.

AI can retrieve approved requirements, summarize relevant context, and help identify possible discrepancies.

AI does not make final design decisions.

## **15\. Review Design With the Client**

When presenting design, connect decisions back to the approved objectives.

Help the client understand why the experience is structured the way it is, how the hierarchy supports the buyer, how messaging and proof are being used, and how the design supports the intended actions.

Client feedback may be a fact correction, preference, editorial change, strategic change, implementation requirement, scope change, or design preference.

Those are not all the same thing.

A client preference can be completely valid without becoming SimpliCreative methodology.

A client request can also have strategic, technical, or scope consequences that need to be discussed before implementation.

Capture material decisions and approvals.

## **16\. Prepare for Development**

Development should begin with an intentional handoff.

Before development starts, confirm the team has:

Approved designs.

Appropriate approved content.

Page requirements.

Functional requirements.

Responsive requirements.

CMS requirements.

Integration requirements.

Form requirements.

CRM requirements.

Measurement requirements.

SEO/AEO/GEO requirements where applicable.

Schema requirements.

Accessibility requirements.

Migration requirements.

Redirect requirements.

Dependencies.

Known unresolved items.

Constraints.

Known accepted limitations.

If something is intentionally unresolved, say so and assign an owner.

The Developer should not have to infer strategic requirements from Figma alone.

## **17\. Build the Website**

Development is human-owned.

Developers determine how approved requirements should be implemented within the project's scope and technical constraints.

AI can help retrieve requirements, summarize specifications, document technical decisions, compare implementation with requirements, and assist with defined checks.

AI does not make final technical architecture decisions and does not independently deploy production.

## **18\. Protect Marketer Autonomy**

FAST requires us to consider whether the website is Flexible.

Where appropriate to the engagement, the finished system should allow the marketing team to perform reasonable routine work without unnecessary technical dependency.

That may include managing content, updating relevant pages, creating campaign experiences, and using approved reusable components.

Flexible does not mean unlimited CMS control.

The implementation should balance marketer autonomy with maintainability, performance, security, brand integrity, technical requirements, scope, and the client's actual capabilities.

## **19\. Build for Accessibility**

Accessibility is part of implementation quality.

Use appropriate automated tools to help identify potential issues, but do not treat an automated scan as a definitive compliance determination.

Human review remains necessary for relevant accessibility requirements.

The goal is an experience that people can effectively access and use.

## **20\. Implement Discoverability Requirements**

Implement the SEO, AEO, and GEO requirements established by the approved strategy.

Depending on the engagement, this may affect information architecture, content, metadata, internal linking, structured data, technical search requirements, authority signals, expertise signals, content relationships, and other approved implementation areas.

Do not add tactics simply because they are popular or considered generic best practices.

The implementation should connect to the client's actual strategy and requirements.

## **21\. Implement Measurement**

Measurement should answer meaningful questions about marketing performance, not simply track whatever is easiest to count.

Implement the approved Measurement Strategy.

Confirm the meaningful conversions, required events, forms, CRM handoffs, UTM requirements, reporting requirements, systems of record, known attribution limitations, and required baselines.

Where the available systems cannot support a reliable connection between marketing activity and business outcomes, preserve that limitation rather than manufacturing certainty.

The goal is to make marketing's impact more visible.

## **22\. Manage Feedback and Changes**

As the project progresses, distinguish between normal implementation feedback and changes that affect strategy or scope.

| What Changed? | What We Do |
| ----- | ----- |
| A detail needs clarification | Resolve it within the team |
| The team needs to choose how to implement an approved requirement | Appropriate specialist decides |
| The client wants something materially outside the agreed work | Bring it to the Project Lead for scope review |
| The change affects what we are building, why, for whom, priority, or success | Bring it to the Strategist |

Do not absorb material additional work simply because it appeared during the project.

Do not turn every minor adjustment into a change order either.

Use the approved scope and professional judgment.

## **23\. Use AI as Support, Not the Decision Maker**

During Foundation, AI is useful for retrieving approved client context, synthesizing research and data, organizing Voice of Customer evidence, preparing first drafts, assembling page requirements and creative briefs, summarizing meetings, organizing client feedback, comparing revisions, identifying possible strategic drift, checking claims, supporting QA, and documenting meaningful decisions.

People still own strategy, client relationships, project management, design, technical architecture, scope, pricing, approvals, QA, and launch.

Anything AI creates for external delivery receives human review first.

## **24\. Keep Project Management in the Project Management System**

monday.com is the current system of record for project execution.

Use it for tasks, owners, deadlines, dependencies, execution status, and operational coordination.

Figma remains the design environment.

MarkUp.io remains the contextual client-feedback environment.

WordPress or the approved development environment remains the implementation environment.

The Second Brain is there to preserve meaningful context and intelligence, not duplicate every activity happening in those systems.

## **25\. Prepare for QA**

Do not use formal QA as the point where we discover that major implementation work is still unfinished.

Before QA begins, confirm that required pages are implemented, required content is present, placeholders are identified, functionality exists, forms and integrations are sufficiently complete for testing, measurement is ready for validation, responsive implementation is ready, known blockers are documented, and required approvals are complete or clearly identified as pending.

Then begin the two QA passes that matter most: strategic fidelity and technical quality.

## **26\. Check Strategic Fidelity**

Strategic Fidelity QA asks:

“Did we build what the approved strategy required?”

Compare the finished experience against the approved strategy, messaging, recommendations, page requirements, conversion requirements, measurement requirements, discoverability requirements, claims, and client decisions.

Look for situations where something technically works but no longer reflects the strategy.

For example, a page may be beautifully designed but bury the primary differentiation. A form may work correctly but fail to capture what measurement requires. A sitemap may be complete but no longer support the approved buyer journey.

Resolve material discrepancies before launch or explicitly document an approved exception.

Do not change the strategy after the fact simply to make implementation appear aligned.

## **27\. Complete Technical QA**

Technical QA validates that the implementation works as intended.

Depending on scope, check responsive behavior, navigation, links, forms, CRM routing, functionality, integrations, analytics, events, SEO implementation, schema, redirects, accessibility requirements, performance, content, browser/device behavior, migration, error states, and other approved technical requirements.

The appropriate human specialist owns final validation.

AI can help find discrepancies.

AI does not approve QA.

## **28\. Decide What Must Be Fixed Before Launch**

Not every QA item has the same impact.

Use four practical categories:

| Classification | Meaning |
| ----- | ----- |
| Blocking | Prevents required functionality or creates unacceptable launch risk |
| Material | Should be resolved before launch unless explicitly accepted |
| Minor | Does not prevent launch and can be resolved according to project judgment |
| Enhancement | Outside the required launch state or potentially outside scope |

Use the project's actual requirements and risk to classify issues.

Do not manage launch readiness by defect count alone.

## **29\. Authorize Launch**

Launch is a human decision.

Before approving production launch, confirm that required strategic and client approvals are complete, strategic fidelity has been checked, technical QA has been completed, forms and integrations work, measurement is ready, redirects and discoverability requirements are addressed, accessibility requirements have been reviewed, content is ready, production configuration is ready, known limitations are documented, and post-launch responsibilities are understood.

Any accepted launch limitation should be explicitly documented with its owner, impact, and follow-up requirement where applicable.

The responsible human authorizes launch.

AI does not.

## **30\. Validate the Live Experience**

Production can behave differently from staging.

After launch, promptly validate the business-critical parts of the live experience.

Depending on scope, check key pages, forms, CRM routing, analytics events, conversion tracking, redirects, search accessibility, integrations, responsive behavior, and other critical functionality.

Resolve launch-critical issues quickly.

Create or complete the Launch Record with the approved launch state, known accepted limitations, unresolved items, and required follow-up responsibilities.

## **31\. Capture Outcomes Without Overclaiming**

Where the engagement includes post-launch measurement, capture what actually happened.

Record what changed, what was measured, the relevant time period, the observed result, data quality, known confounding factors, what appears to be working, what remains uncertain, and what cannot responsibly be concluded.

Do not automatically attribute short-term performance changes to the website.

Do not claim ROI unless the available revenue, cost, and attribution evidence can responsibly support it.

Evidence determines certainty.

## **32\. Transition Ongoing Ownership**

At Foundation completion, determine who owns the website and related marketing responsibilities going forward.

The next path may be:

```
SIMPLICARE
```

or

```
CLIENT / PARTNER OWNERSHIP
```

SimpliCARE is appropriate when the client wants an ongoing relationship within the current CARE offer architecture.

It is not mandatory after Foundation.

Clarify who owns routine website changes, technical maintenance, measurement, content, marketing systems, ongoing optimization, and any unresolved items.

For either transition path, create an implementation handoff that identifies:

The current production state.

Approved strategy and requirements that remain relevant.

Known limitations.

Unresolved items.

Measurement requirements.

Ongoing responsibilities.

Relevant systems and assets.

Required access or ownership transfers.

The receiving team should not have to reconstruct Foundation decisions from project history.

The client should not leave Foundation uncertain about who is responsible for what next.

## **33\. Close the Foundation**

Before closing the engagement, confirm:

| Closeout Check | Complete |
| ----- | ----- |
| Approved scope delivered | Required |
| Required client approvals complete | Required |
| Strategic Fidelity QA complete | Required |
| Technical QA complete | Required |
| Launch complete | Where applicable |
| Critical post-launch checks complete | Where applicable |
| Material decisions documented | Required |
| Material feedback captured | Required |
| Approved messaging and content stored | Where applicable |
| Known limitations documented | Required |
| Ongoing ownership documented | Required |
| Implementation handoff completed | Required |
| Durable client knowledge updated | Required |
| Relevant outcomes captured | Where applicable |
| SimpliCARE or client/partner ownership established | Required |

Foundation is not complete simply because the website is live.

It is complete when the agreed work has been delivered, validated, handed off, and appropriately documented.

The Project Lead confirms operational completion of the engagement.

The Strategist confirms that material strategic requirements have been implemented, intentionally changed through an approved decision, or documented as an accepted limitation.

The appropriate specialists confirm completion and QA within their areas of responsibility.

Where the client or an external partner assumes ongoing ownership, the Project Lead confirms that the required implementation context, known limitations, unresolved items, and ongoing responsibilities have been handed off.

## **34\. Decide What Is Worth Remembering**

Not everything that happens during Foundation belongs in the Second Brain.

Preserve information that is likely to matter later, such as approved strategy, messaging, durable client facts and preferences, material decisions, implementation requirements, approved claims, important corrections, scope changes, accepted limitations, launch state, outcome evidence, and meaningful human judgment.

Do not permanently capture every task, comment, Figma adjustment, MarkUp.io annotation, minor copy edit, routine development choice, or project-status change.

The question is:

“Will knowing this later help us understand this client, this decision, this implementation, or a meaningful outcome?”

If not, it probably belongs in the operational system rather than durable intelligence.

## **35\. Learn Without Overgeneralizing**

Foundation will teach us things.

A client may respond strongly to a particular recommendation. A design approach may work well. A technical choice may prove useful. A strategist may override an AI recommendation for an important reason.

Capture meaningful learning.

Do not turn one client's experience into an agency rule.

Reusable SimpliCreative methodology should emerge from repeated evidence, human reflection, and deliberate approval.

The goal is to become smarter through delivery without allowing individual projects to silently rewrite how SimpliCreative works.

## **Foundation Delivery at a Glance**

| Phase | Team Focus | Done When |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Activate | Scope, strategy, ownership, access | Team has a valid starting point |
| Handoff | Translate strategy into implementation context | Delivery team understands what and why |
| Plan | Define workstreams, dependencies, responsibilities | Execution path is clear |
| Prepare | Messaging, IA, page requirements, content, creative brief | Design has what it needs |
| Design | UX and visual expression | Client-approved design is ready |
| Develop | Build approved experience | Implementation is ready for QA |
| Validate | Strategic Fidelity \+ Technical QA | Required issues are resolved or accepted |
| Launch | Production deployment and live checks | Critical live experience works |
| Transition | Ownership, CARE, documentation, implementation handoff | Next responsibilities are clear |
| Learn | Capture durable intelligence and outcomes | Useful knowledge survives the project |

The standard for Foundation is not simply:

“Did we launch the website?”

It is:

“Did the strategy survive execution, did we deliver what we agreed to deliver, and did we leave the client with a digital foundation their marketing team can actually use?”
