## **1\. Purpose**

This guide defines how SimpliCreative communicates.

It governs both human-written and AI-assisted communication and should be used whenever content is created on behalf of the SimpliCreative brand.

The goal is not simply to make SimpliCreative sound polished or professional.

The goal is to help the audience see their marketing more clearly, recognize connections they may have missed, understand the business implications, and make better decisions.

Our primary editorial principle is:

“We don’t persuade by being louder. We persuade by helping people see something they hadn’t connected before.”

## **3\. Brand Voice**

SimpliCreative sounds like a trusted strategic advisor who sees the connections others miss and can translate those connections into action.

The voice is calm, strategic, perceptive, executive, specific, confident, practical, collaborative, evidence-aware, human, and memorable.

SimpliCreative is not loud, overly clever, academic, hype-driven, coachy, generic, or filled with consultant jargon.

We do not create urgency for urgency’s sake.

We create clarity.

The reader should come away thinking:

“They understand what I’m dealing with.”

“I hadn’t connected those things before.”

“That explains why what we’ve been doing isn’t working as well as it should.”

“There is a logical way to figure out what to do next.”

## **4\. Voice Characteristics**

| Characteristic | What It Means | What It Does Not Mean |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Strategic | Connect the immediate issue to the larger marketing system | Abstract strategy language |
| Perceptive | Surface something the reader may not have connected | Trying to sound clever |
| Calm | Explain risk without manufacturing anxiety | Passive or timid |
| Executive | Connect marketing decisions to business consequences | Corporate jargon |
| Specific | Use recognizable situations and concrete implications | Generic marketing language |
| Confident | Take a position when evidence supports it | Overclaiming |
| Practical | Move insight toward a useful decision | Tactical tips for their own sake |
| Collaborative | Respect client and partner expertise | Agreeing with everything |
| Evidence-Aware | Distinguish fact, observation, hypothesis, and opinion | Hedging every statement |
| Human | Sound like an experienced person talking to another experienced person | Casual internet slang |
| Memorable | Express important ideas in language worth remembering | Forced cleverness |

## **5\. Strategic Architect Perspective**

SimpliCreative communicates from the perspective of a Strategic Architect.

A Strategic Architect does not begin with:

“What should we build?”

They begin with:

“What is actually creating the friction?”

They understand the relationships between business strategy, positioning, messaging, website experience, content, discoverability, demand generation, credibility, conversion, technology, sales, and measurement.

Then they determine what needs to change first.

Whenever appropriate, communication should help the reader move through:

Symptom → Connection → Likely Cause → Business Implication → Better Decision

Avoid:

Problem → SimpliCreative Service

## **6\. Recognition Before Persuasion**

Create recognition before asking the audience to act.

Start with a situation the reader recognizes.

Then reveal the connection beneath it.

Then explain why the connection matters.

Then recommend an appropriate next move.

Preferred progression:

Recognizable Situation → Unexpected Connection → Business Consequence → Clear Recommendation

The objective is not merely to create an “I agree” response.

Strong SimpliCreative communication should create an “I hadn’t thought about it that way” moment.

## **7\. Diagnose Before You Prescribe**

Do not immediately prescribe a website redesign, more content, another tool, a new channel, SEO, AEO/GEO, CRO, paid media, or another tactic simply because the visible symptom points there.

First determine:

What is happening?

What does the client or audience believe is causing it?

What evidence supports that?

What else could contribute?

How are the relevant parts of the marketing ecosystem connected?

What do we still not know?

What needs to be understood before making the implementation decision?

Respect the symptom while investigating the cause.

## **8\. Lead With the Problem They Recognize**

SimpliCreative may understand the systemic problem before the prospect does.

Do not require the prospect to understand SimpliCreative terminology before they can understand the message.

Start with recognizable friction.

Examples:

“You’re generating traffic, but not enough qualified demand.”

“Your paid campaigns keep getting more expensive.”

“Leadership wants clearer evidence that marketing is working.”

“Your team has a strategy, but execution still feels fragmented.”

“Your website technically works, but every campaign seems to require another workaround.”

Then reveal the larger connection.

Avoid leading with abstract language such as:

“Your interconnected digital ecosystem lacks strategic orchestration.”

## **9\. Reframe Without Dismissing**

A signature SimpliCreative thought-leadership structure is:

“You think you have a \_\_\_ problem. You may actually have a \_\_\_ problem.”

The visible problem is still real.

Do not tell the audience they are wrong.

Show them that the visible problem may be downstream from something larger.

The objective is:

Respect the Symptom → Reveal the Connection → Investigate the Cause

## **10\. The Website’s Role**

Do not describe the website as simply another marketing channel.

Preferred framing includes:

“Your website isn’t just another marketing channel. It’s the connective layer between them.”

“Your website should be the place where your marketing comes together.”

“Marketing is more fragmented than ever. Your foundation shouldn’t be.”

The website can connect positioning, messaging, content, PR, paid media, search and AI discoverability, sales enablement, CRM, analytics, conversion, and buyer experience.

Do not imply that the website controls every marketing function.

The website is the controlled digital environment where much of the marketing ecosystem connects.

## **11\. Business Impact Language**

Connect marketing work to business impact without making claims the available evidence cannot support.

Preferred language includes:

Marketing impact

Marketing performance

Marketing investment

Business outcomes

Qualified demand

Buyer trust

Conversion

Visibility

Influence

Contribution

Executive clarity

Measurable impact

Use stronger financial language only when sufficient revenue, margin, attribution, and cost evidence exists.

Do not automatically equate marketing activity with revenue or ROI.

“Prove impact” is broader and safer than promising “prove ROI” when the evidence cannot support true ROI attribution.

## **12\. Executive Action Principles**

When communicating with senior marketing leaders and executives, make the decision easier to understand without manipulating the reader.

| Principle | SimpliCreative Application | Avoid |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Cost of Inaction | Show legitimate waste, friction, risk, lost opportunity, or reduced visibility created by the current situation | Invented financial losses |
| Peer Validation | Use credible examples or benchmarks when evidence exists | Manufactured FOMO |
| Executive Risk | Recognize accountability, leadership expectations, and pressure to justify investment | Exploiting career fear |
| Friction Reduction | Provide a clear recommended next step | Overwhelming menus of choices |
| Autonomy | Recommend and explain | Hard-close language |
| Control | Show how better systems improve visibility and decision-making | Fear-based “losing control” messaging |

Behavioral psychology is a reasoning aid, not a persuasion script.

Use it to clarify stakes, reduce decision friction, provide credible validation, and preserve buyer autonomy.

Never manufacture fear, scarcity, competitive pressure, financial loss, social proof, or career risk.

## **13\. Loss Framing Without Fear**

When appropriate, frame the cost of inaction around investment, effort, capacity, or opportunity already at risk.

Preferred:

“Before you invest another dollar in marketing, make sure your website isn’t holding everything else back.”

“More marketing doesn’t solve a foundation that isn’t supporting the marketing you already have.”

“Every disconnected handoff makes a lean team work harder to produce the same result.”

“Before adding another channel, determine whether the channels you already have are reinforcing one another.”

Avoid:

“Your competitors are passing you by.”

“Every day you wait is costing you revenue.”

“Your website is killing your pipeline.”

Make structural risk visible without inventing a threat.

## **14\. Executive Decision Clarity**

Do not create unnecessary decision fatigue.

When sufficient evidence exists to make a recommendation, make one.

Avoid presenting five possibilities when one clearly deserves priority.

Preferred:

“Based on what we’re seeing, I would address X first because Y.”

For executive recommendations, use:

Situation → Evidence → Implication → Recommendation → Decision Required

Provide enough context to understand the recommendation without forcing the reader to reconstruct the analysis.

## **15\. Preserve Buyer Autonomy**

SimpliCreative diagnoses and recommends.

The buyer retains agency.

Preferred language includes:

“Based on the evidence, we recommend…”

“The first issue we would resolve is…”

“Before making that investment, we would validate…”

“The evidence points us toward…”

“We see two contributing issues, but one needs to be resolved first…”

Avoid:

“You have no choice but to…”

“The only solution is…”

“You’d be crazy not to…”

Aggressive hard-close language.

A strong recommendation should feel like informed guidance, not pressure.

## **16\. Write to a Person, Not an ICP Label**

Before drafting important communication, picture the actual person receiving it.

“Marketing leader” is not enough context.

A useful writing context might be:

“A VP of Marketing with six people on her team, three agencies in her inbox, a paid campaign launching next month, a website she can’t easily change, and a leadership meeting Friday where she is expected to explain marketing performance.”

The final copy does not need to contain all those details.

The writer needs enough situational context to understand the person behind the ICP.

Write to one person while maintaining standards appropriate for the full audience.

## **17\. Specificity Over Bizspeak**

If a sentence could appear on almost any agency or consultancy website, challenge it.

Generic:

“We create integrated digital experiences that drive measurable business outcomes.”

More SimpliCreative:

“Your paid media, content, PR, sales team, and website shouldn’t be telling five slightly different versions of the same story.”

Generic:

“We align strategy and execution.”

More specific:

“The strategy shouldn’t disappear somewhere between the deck, the agency handoff, and the website that actually gets built.”

Prefer plain language and recognizable situations over abstract marketing terminology.

## **18\. Earn the Memorable Line**

Not every sentence needs personality.

Every important piece should contain at least one idea expressed in language worth remembering.

Strong existing examples include:

“Marketing works better when it works together.”

“Marketing is more fragmented than ever. Your foundation shouldn’t be.”

“Your website isn’t just another marketing channel. It’s the connective layer between them.”

“Stop solving connected marketing problems one tactic at a time.”

“Diagnose before you prescribe.”

Memorability should usually come from a sharp strategic observation rather than forced humor or cleverness.

Do not bury the strongest idea beneath excessive setup.

## **19\. Personality and Humanity**

SimpliCreative communication should sound like a thoughtful human wrote it.

Personality may come from a sharp observation, unexpected contrast, knowing aside, vivid example, concise metaphor, occasional humor when natural, or conversational turn of phrase.

Do not add jokes simply to make professional writing feel human.

Do not force quirkiness.

Humanity should support credibility, not compete with it.

## **20\. Surprise Through Insight**

The preferred form of surprise is strategic reversal.

Examples:

“You think you have a traffic problem. You may have a credibility problem.”

“You think your paid ads need fixing. Your landing experience may be making the ads work harder than they should.”

“You don’t necessarily need another marketing channel. You may need the channels you already have to work together.”

The surprise should reveal something useful.

Avoid novelty for novelty’s sake.

## **21\. Use Tension Without Drama**

Good SimpliCreative writing often contains a meaningful contradiction.

Examples:

Strategy exists, but execution is fragmented.

Marketing activity is increasing, but impact is not becoming clearer.

Traffic exists, but qualified demand does not follow.

PR creates attention, but the website does not capture it.

Marketing has more tools, but the team has less agility.

Use the tension to create curiosity.

Resolve it through insight rather than melodrama.

## **22\. Make the Stakes Concrete**

Do not stop at:

“This affects marketing performance.”

Explain how.

Depending on the evidence, consequences may include:

More spend required to create the same result.

More manual coordination for a lean team.

Longer campaign launch times.

Inconsistent messaging across channels.

Attention arriving at a weak conversion experience.

Leadership unable to see marketing’s contribution.

Sales receiving poorly qualified opportunities.

PR or paid investment losing momentum after the click.

Content failing to support buyer progression.

The more concrete the consequence, the less hype the communication needs.

## **23\. Strategy and Execution Under One Roof**

One of the strongest reasons clients choose SimpliCreative is:

“Strategy and execution under one roof.”

Explain the consequence rather than relying on the phrase alone.

SimpliCreative combines executive-level strategic thinking with hands-on implementation.

This can reduce handoffs, coordination burden, translation loss, and the risk that strategic intent disappears during execution.

Do not position SimpliCreative as doing everything.

SimpliCreative collaborates with existing PR, paid media, SEO, CRM, content, sales, and other partners when appropriate.

## **24\. What SimpliCreative Is Not**

Do not position SimpliCreative as:

A production shop.

A generic web-design vendor.

A full-service agency that must own every channel.

A one-tactic specialist.

A deck-only consultancy.

A “more activity” marketing agency.

A growth-hack agency.

A replacement for every existing marketing partner.

We diagnose what is creating the friction, determine what needs to change, and connect strategy with execution.

## **27\. Sentence Style**

Favor clear, direct sentences.

Use plain English before marketing terminology.

Vary sentence length.

Use short sentences for important ideas.

Use longer sentences when they help explain a meaningful connection.

Prefer active voice.

Use contractions when natural.

Do not overuse rhetorical questions.

Do not make every paragraph a one-line statement.

Avoid excessive parentheticals.

Avoid em dashes in SimpliCreative brand content. Use periods, commas, colons, or parentheses instead.

Do not use exclamation points as a substitute for confidence.

## **28\. Headlines**

Headlines should communicate an insight, consequence, recognizable problem, or clear outcome.

Preferred patterns include:

“Before you invest another dollar in marketing, make sure your website isn’t holding everything else back.”

“Marketing is more fragmented than ever. Your foundation shouldn’t be.”

“Stop solving connected marketing problems one tactic at a time.”

“Your website isn’t just another marketing channel. It’s the connective layer between them.”

“More marketing activity doesn’t automatically create more marketing impact.”

Avoid vague headlines such as:

“Unlock Your Digital Potential.”

“Transform Your Marketing.”

“Build a Better Future.”

“Take Your Business to the Next Level.”

## **29\. Thought Leadership**

Thought leadership should primarily help the audience recognize and understand problems before pushing solutions.

A useful content balance is approximately 60 to 70 percent awareness and problem-aware content.

Priority territories include:

More activity does not necessarily create proportionally more impact.

The hidden cost of a website that technically works but does not support the marketing system.

Why proving marketing impact becomes difficult when the buyer journey and data are fragmented.

How buyer discovery is changing across search, AI, social, PR, paid, referral, events, and sales.

Why lean teams often need less fragmentation rather than more tools and vendors.

Why a visible channel problem may originate somewhere else in the ecosystem.

Thought leadership should make connections, not simply publish tactical checklists.

## **30\. Content Structure**

The primary SimpliCreative editorial progression is:

Recognizable Situation → Tension → Connection → Insight → Evidence → Business Consequence → Recommendation → Invitation

Not every piece needs every element.

Use the sequence as a reasoning framework, not a rigid writing template.

Do not begin by announcing SimpliCreative’s expertise.

Help the reader recognize what is happening, reveal the connection beneath it, make the consequence understandable, and show the logical next move.

## **31\. Format Follows the Idea**

Do not assume every insight belongs in the same content format.

Choose the format based on the communication objective.

A single strategic observation may work as a social post.

A connected-problem explanation may work better as a diagram.

A diagnostic framework may work better as a Scorecard.

A complex executive argument may need an article or presentation.

A comparison may need a table.

A decision process may need a flowchart.

AI may recommend a format.

Humans make the final format decision.

## **32\. Calls to Action**

Calls to action should match buyer readiness.

Do not force every reader into a sales conversation.

Examples include:

Take the Lean Marketing Scorecard™

Validate Your Next Move

Request a Consultation

Explore the Framework

See How It Works

Review the Approach

Start With the Scorecard

Talk Through the Problem

The CTA should feel like the logical next step after the insight that preceded it.

## **33\. Claims and Evidence**

Distinguish clearly between observed fact, client-reported information, evidence-supported conclusion, working hypothesis, and opinion or point of view.

Do not present hypotheses as facts.

Do not invent statistics, results, customer claims, market claims, pricing, guarantees, rankings, competitor behavior, or financial consequences.

Use verified evidence when making factual claims.

If evidence is missing, qualify the statement or flag it for validation.

AI-generated confidence is not evidence.

## **34\. Human Read-Aloud Test**

Before approving important communication, read it aloud.

Ask:

Would a smart human actually say this?

Does it sound overly polished or synthetic?

Are the sentences all the same length?

Does it contain unnecessary marketing vocabulary?

Are there phrases no one would naturally use?

Does it sound alive or wooden?

Does it sound like SimpliCreative or like competent generic AI?

Revise when necessary.

## **35\. Distinctiveness Test**

Remove the SimpliCreative name from the draft.

Would an informed reader still recognize the thinking?

If not, determine whether the piece is missing SimpliCreative’s point of view.

Distinctiveness should primarily come from how SimpliCreative sees the problem:

Connected problems over isolated tactics.

Diagnosis before prescription.

Website as a connective digital foundation.

Strategy and execution staying connected.

Lean-team realities.

Marketing impact becoming visible.

Less fragmentation rather than automatically more activity.

Distinctiveness should come from thinking first and word choice second.

## **36\. AI Context Requirements**

AI should not begin substantive messaging from brand voice alone.

Voice controls expression.

Strategy controls meaning.

Before generating important messaging, retrieve the relevant approved context for audience, situation, objective, buyer stage, problem, strategic insight, evidence, offer, claims, CTA, and format.

If material context is missing, surface the gap rather than inventing strategy to complete the draft.

## **37\. AI Drafting Sequence**

For substantive messaging, use:

Approved Strategy → Audience & Situational Context → Relevant Evidence → Approved Messaging Framework(s) → SimpliCreative Brand Voice & Editorial Standards → Deliverable Requirements → Draft → Evidence Check → Strategic Alignment Check → Editorial Quality Check → Human Review

Do not ask AI to compensate for missing strategic inputs by becoming more creative.

Missing strategy should be surfaced, not disguised with better prose.

## **38\. AI Editorial Quality Check**

Before presenting a substantive draft for human review, evaluate:

| Check | Question |
| ----- | ----- |
| Real Person | Can I picture the specific person this is written for? |
| Recognition | Will that person recognize their actual situation? |
| Insight | Is there an “aha,” or merely information they already know? |
| Connection | Have I revealed a meaningful relationship between problems? |
| Strongest Idea | Did I bury the most important insight under setup? |
| Specificity | Could this copy belong to almost any consultancy? |
| Plain Language | Can jargon or bizspeak be removed? |
| Stakes | Is the business consequence concrete and supportable? |
| Evidence | Are factual claims supported? |
| Loss Framing | Am I showing legitimate risk rather than manufacturing fear? |
| Autonomy | Does the reader retain control of the decision? |
| Recommendation | Is the next step clear without unnecessary choices? |
| Memorability | Is an important idea expressed in language worth remembering? |
| Humanity | Does this sound written by an intelligent human rather than polished AI? |
| Voice | Is it calm, strategic, perceptive, practical, and confident? |
| Restraint | Does it avoid exaggeration, hype, and false certainty? |
| CTA | Does the requested action match buyer readiness? |

## **38\. AI Pattern Detection**

## AI Pattern Detection is a mandatory pre-delivery check for substantive AI-generated SimpliCreative copy.

## AI-generated writing can develop predictable patterns that make content feel synthetic, generic, or formulaic. Before presenting a substantive draft for human review, AI should complete a dedicated pattern-detection pass and revise material instances it finds.

## This check happens before the broader AI Editorial Quality Check. First clean the machinery, then evaluate the craft.

### **1\. The Three-Sentence Paragraph Trap**

## AI frequently produces paragraphs with the same rhythm: setup, expansion, conclusion.

## When repeated, the writing becomes mechanically predictable.

## Paragraph length should vary naturally according to the idea. Some paragraphs may be one sentence. Others may develop an argument across several sentences. A deliberate fragment may occasionally be appropriate.

## If three consecutive paragraphs are all exactly three sentences long, treat this as a pattern-detection flag and revise the cadence.

## Do not vary paragraph length randomly. The rhythm should serve the meaning.

### **2\. Surface-Level Content**

## AI often produces statements that are technically true but too obvious to be useful or memorable.

## Challenge sentences that merely state conventional wisdom without adding insight.

## Go one level deeper by introducing a specific situation, consequence, tension, example, evidence point, meaningful connection, or way of seeing the problem the reader may not have considered.

## Ask:

## “Does this sentence tell the reader something useful, or does it merely sound reasonable?”

## “If I changed the company name, could this sentence appear on almost any competitor’s website?”

## If the answer to the second question is yes, strengthen, specify, or remove it.

## Specificity must remain evidence-aware. AI should not invent statistics, scenarios presented as facts, consequences, or other details merely to make generic writing sound more specific.

### **3\. Em Dash Overload**

## SimpliCreative avoids em dashes in brand content.

## AI frequently uses em dashes as a substitute for stronger sentence structure or to insert unnecessary asides.

## During pattern detection, replace em dashes with periods, commas, colons, parentheses, or rewritten sentence structure.

## Do not preserve an em dash merely because it makes the sentence sound more conversational.

## If an em dash is intentionally desired for a specific piece, a human editor may approve the exception.

### **4\. AI Filler Phrases**

## Flag formulaic language that adds little meaning or has become strongly associated with generic AI-generated copy.

## Common examples include:

## “In today’s landscape,” “in today’s environment,” or “in today’s world.”

## “Navigate,” when the intended meaning is simply handle, manage, address, or deal with.

## “Unlock.”

## “Empower.”

## “Elevate.”

## “Seamless.”

## “Robust.”

## “Game-changer.”

## “At the end of the day.”

## “The reality is.”

## “Here’s the thing.”

## “Let’s be honest.”

## “Simply put.”

## “That’s where \[brand\] comes in.”

## “That’s why we built…”

## “Think of it as…”

## “In other words…”

## When one appears, first test whether it can simply be deleted.

## If meaning is lost, replace it with language that communicates the specific idea.

## These phrases are pattern flags, not an absolute prohibition against every possible contextual use. Deliberate, specific language should govern the final decision.

### **5\. Contrast-Framing Tic**

## AI frequently uses contrast constructions to manufacture the appearance of insight.

## Common patterns include:

## “It’s not just X, it’s Y.”

## “More than just X.”

## “Doesn’t just X, it Y’s.”

## “It’s not about X, it’s about Y.”

## “Not X. Y.”

## Contrast is valuable when the difference itself reveals something meaningful.

## Repeated contrast framing becomes formulaic.

## One genuinely useful contrast may be appropriate within a piece. Repeated use should trigger revision.

## When the pattern appears, test whether the stronger claim can stand on its own.

## Do not manufacture a contrast simply to make ordinary information sound like a revelation.

### **Pattern Detection Guardrail**

## The governing rule is:

## `AI_PATTERN_DETECTION_REQUIRED_BEFORE_HUMAN_REVIEW`

## Pattern detection should improve naturalness without overriding strategy, evidence, clarity, accessibility, or the intended audience.

## AI must not introduce unsupported specificity merely to make writing feel more human.

## AI must not deliberately add errors, awkward grammar, random fragments, slang, or artificial imperfections to disguise AI involvement.

## The objective is not to make AI-generated writing look deceptively human.

## The objective is to remove formulaic writing patterns and produce communication that meets SimpliCreative’s editorial standard.

### **Originality & Source Integrity Check**

## After AI Pattern Detection and before the broader AI Editorial Quality Check, substantive AI-assisted content must comply with the Originality & Source Integrity Standard defined in `01_Governance_and_Intelligence_Architecture.md`.

## Reference materials may inform reasoning, methodology, structure, depth, and quality. They must not be treated as copy banks.

## Potentially material source similarity, uncertain attribution, or questionable reuse must be surfaced for human review using:

## `POTENTIAL_SOURCE_SIMILARITY_REQUIRES_HUMAN_REVIEW`

## AI must not certify an output as legally “copyright safe,” “plagiarism free,” or guaranteed original.

## The final sequence in `03` should now be:

## `Approved Strategy → Context & Evidence → Frameworks → Brand Voice → Draft → Evidence Check → Strategic Alignment Check → AI Pattern Detection → Originality & Source Integrity Check → AI Editorial Quality Check → Human Review`

## **39\. AI Use of Behavioral Psychology**

Behavioral frameworks may inform messaging strategy but must not become manipulation scripts.

AI may use approved behavioral principles to clarify legitimate stakes, explain the cost of inaction when evidence supports it, reduce unnecessary decision friction, present a clear recommendation, use credible peer validation, connect decisions to stated executive priorities, and preserve autonomy.

AI must not invent fear, manufacture scarcity, invent competitor behavior, fabricate social proof, invent financial loss, exploit personal career anxiety, create artificial urgency, remove meaningful choice, or use psychological principles to override the reader’s interests.

The purpose of behavioral insight is to make good decisions easier to understand and act on.

## **40\. Human Editing and Learning**

AI drafts are starting points.

Human editing is part of the system.

Meaningful human or client edits may reveal a strategic decision, editorial principle, client preference, factual correction, buyer insight, stronger framing, or reusable reasoning pattern.

Do not treat every edit as new methodology.

Client-specific preferences remain client-specific unless independently validated and deliberately promoted into agency-level knowledge.

## **41\. Examples and Gold Standards**

Approved examples may teach AI what strong SimpliCreative communication looks like.

Examples demonstrate application, structure, reasoning patterns, and quality standards.

Examples are not policy.

Examples do not override current strategy, methodology, this guide, or task-specific evidence.

Historical examples must remain clearly identified as historical.

## **42\. Internal vs. Client-Facing Terminology**

Retired client-facing vocabulary should not be used in public brand messaging simply because it appears in internal architecture.

For example, “source of truth” may remain valid as an internal engineering or governance term when describing authoritative records. It should not be used as a public-facing brand phrase if it is marked retired in messaging standards.

AI must apply retired-vocabulary rules according to communication context.

## **43\. Final SimpliCreative Test**

Before approving important communication, ask:

Does this help the audience see a meaningful connection they may have been missing?

Will the intended reader recognize themselves and their situation?

Is there a genuine insight rather than generic marketing advice?

Does it sound like someone who understands both strategy and execution?

Does it respect the reader’s intelligence?

Does it make the business consequence understandable without exaggerating it?

Does it diagnose before prescribing?

Does it make the appropriate decision clearer?

Does it preserve the reader’s autonomy?

Is the strongest idea memorable?

Could any unsupported claim be mistaken for fact?

Does it create clarity rather than urgency for urgency’s sake?

Does it sound distinctly like SimpliCreative?

If the answer is yes, the communication is likely on brand.
